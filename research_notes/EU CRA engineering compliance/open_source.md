# EU Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) and open-source software — status as of 30 October 2026

Research date: 30 October 2026. All dates below are as stated in the cited source. Primary legal text was read from EUR-Lex (recitals) and, because EUR-Lex fetches truncated before the operative articles, from an article-by-article mirror (enobyte.com) whose paragraph numbering was cross-checked against the Commission's own guidance and the ORC WG/Linux Foundation summaries. Treat mirror text as a close paraphrase of the Official Journal, not the authentic text.

Key dates (Art 71 and Commission sources): entry into force 10 December 2024 per EUR-Lex metadata and the Commission (the ORC WG page says 11 December 2024 — a one-day discrepancy); Chapter IV (Arts 35–51, notification of conformity assessment bodies) applies from 11 June 2026; Art 14 reporting applies from 11 September 2026; everything else — including Art 24 steward obligations — applies from 11 December 2027.

---

## Key question 1 — Scope: when is open-source software in or out of the CRA?

### Takeaway
Article 2 never mentions open source; the exclusion works through the definition of "making available on the market" (Art 3(22)), which requires supply "in the course of a commercial activity", read with recitals 15–20 and now with Section 3 (points 40–88) of the Commission's final guidance C(2026) 5252 of 27 July 2026. Unmonetised FOSS is outside the CRA unless its publisher is a legal person qualifying as an open-source software steward, in which case only Art 24 applies; monetised FOSS makes the publisher a manufacturer.

### Cited Findings
- Art 2(1): the Regulation applies to products with digital elements made available on the market whose intended or reasonably foreseeable use includes a direct or indirect logical or physical data connection; Art 2 contains no reference to free and open-source software or "commercial activity" in any of its eight paragraphs — [Enobyte mirror, Art 2](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/2/)
- Art 3(22) "making available on the market" = supply for distribution or use on the Union market "in the course of a commercial activity", whether in return for payment or free of charge; Art 3(21) "placing on the market" = first making available; Art 3(13) "manufacturer" = a natural or legal person who develops/manufactures (or has developed/manufactured) a product and markets it under its own name or trademark, whether for payment, monetisation or free of charge; Art 3(48) "free and open-source software" = software whose source code is openly shared and which is made available under a free and open-source licence providing all rights to make it freely accessible, usable, modifiable and redistributable — [Enobyte mirror, Art 3](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/3/)
- Recital 15: supply is commercial where, for example, a price is charged for technical support beyond cost recovery, or where use of the product requires processing personal data for reasons other than security, compatibility or interoperability — [EUR-Lex PDF, recitals](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)
- Recital 16 (public administrations): a fee charged solely to recover the actual costs of a service does not, on that ground alone, make the supply a commercial activity — [EUR-Lex PDF, recitals](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)
- Recital 18: FOSS products "that are not monetised by their manufacturers" are not a commercial activity; receiving financial support/contributions from manufacturers, or "the mere presence of regular releases", does not in itself make the activity commercial; development by not-for-profit organisations is not commercial provided all earnings after costs are used for not-for-profit objectives; how the product was developed or financed "should therefore not be taken into account" — [EUR-Lex HTML, recitals](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)
- Recital 20: "The sole act of hosting products with digital elements on open repositories", including through package managers or collaboration platforms, is not making available on the market; hosts are distributors only if they supply the software in the course of a commercial activity — [EUR-Lex HTML, recitals](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)
- Commission guidance C(2026) 5252, point 43: FOSS not placed on the market falls outside the CRA unless its publisher is a steward under Art 3(14), in which case only Art 24 applies; points 44–46: FOSS requires both a licence granting the full set of rights and openly shared source code; source shared only with paying customers or a limited group does not qualify; the guidance names no specific licences and does not address dual licensing — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Guidance points 51–53 (charging a price): charging for the software itself (e.g. pre-compiled binaries) places it on the market; paid versions are separate products placed on the market while free "community" versions are not, even if the codebases are nearly identical (open core); a legal person supplying the free community version is a steward for it, a natural person supplying it is outside the CRA — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Guidance point 54: placed on the market where the FOSS is used to monetise other products/services (Examples 14–16: ad/commission-funded marketplace app; free VPN selling extra servers; fitness app conditioned on personal-data processing for ads/analytics) — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Guidance points 55–59 (support services): paid consulting, training or professional services alone do not place FOSS on the market; the "decisive factor" is whether access to the FOSS, including its maintenance, requires payment; paid access to a specific version bundled with technical assistance is placed on the market even if a free equivalent exists (Example 17: paid OS edition vs free OS); natural persons charging only to recover actual costs "including reasonable living expenses" are not placing on the market on that basis alone; providing technical help for FOSS not under your responsibility is not placing it on the market unless you substantially modify it (Art 22; Example 19) — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Guidance points 60–62 (donations): donation links do not by themselves mean placing on the market, even if donations exceed costs; donations become a de facto price where access to the software, essential functions or updates depends on them, or donors receive contractual/exclusive benefits (Examples 20–22) — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Guidance points 63–65 (financing): a third party paying for or sponsoring development (grants, bounties, paid development work) does not by itself determine placing on the market; openly shared FOSS that is not otherwise monetised is not placed on the market and the integrating company owes Art 13(5) due diligence (Example 23) — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Guidance point 66 (not-for-profit entities): a not-for-profit legal person whose earnings after costs go to not-for-profit objectives does not place its FOSS on the market and may be a steward (Example 24: not-for-profit browser monetised via search partnerships is not placed on the market; publisher is a steward) — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Guidance points 67–68 (integration by other manufacturers): FOSS intended for integration by other manufacturers is not placed on the market unless the original publisher monetises it; the publisher is a steward if it sustains the FOSS on an ongoing basis (Example 25: a legal person publishes an unmonetised UI library it integrates into its own products — it is the steward; Example 26: OS vendor's unmonetised reference libraries) — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Law-firm reading of the final guidance (HLC, 31 July 2026): whether FOSS is commercial is assessed product by product, not from the entity's overall business model; "A community edition may remain a distinct, non-monetized product" even if the same entity sells a paid edition built on the same code — [HLC](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/eu-cyber-resilience-act-european-commission-publishes-final-cra-guidance)
- Commission FOSDEM slides (1 Feb 2026, DG CONNECT): only directly monetised open-source products are subject to the full set of obligations; non-commercial FOSS developers have no CRA obligations — [FOSDEM 2026 slides](https://archive.fosdem.org/2026/events/attachments/EERURR-implementing_the_cyber_resilience_act_-_engaging_with_open_source/slides/267519/slides-cr_pyebtat.pdf)
- Center for Cybersecurity Policy and Law (5 Aug 2026): the final guidance clarified that common upstream/downstream code-sharing practices can fall within the FOSS definition, answering a Cybersecurity Coalition concern that the definition was too narrow — [CCPL](https://www.centerforcybersecuritypolicy.org/insights-and-research/european-commission-publishes-final-cyber-resilience-act-implementation-guidance-addresses-concerns-raised-by-cybersecurity-coalition)

### Inferences
- "Developers salaried by a company" is not addressed by name; the closest rule is point 63 (paid development work/sponsorship does not determine placing on the market) plus recital 18 (financing is irrelevant). The salary question therefore collapses into who publishes and controls releases: if the employer publishes the FOSS unmonetised, the employer is a steward (Examples 25, 29); if the employer monetises it, the employer is a manufacturer.
- "Software developed inside a company and released as open source" is likewise not addressed by name; Examples 25 and 29 (company publishes unmonetised FOSS used by others → company is steward, not manufacturer) are the operative analogues.
- Paid hosting of FOSS is not in the support-services sub-section; the hooks would be recital 15/point 54 (monetising other services) — a hosted SaaS offering built on FOSS is a separately monetised service, so the hosting business is likely a manufacturer of its hosted product while the FOSS itself can remain unplaced if freely available. This is my inference, not stated in the guidance.

### Gaps
- Dual licensing is explicitly not addressed in the guidance's FOSS section (per the mirror fetch); the open-core treatment at points 52–53 is the nearest analogue.
- No guidance point on public administrations as publishers or stewards of FOSS was found beyond recital 16.
- The Commission's authoritative PDF (ec.europa.eu/newsroom/dae/redirection/document/131456) could not be read by the fetch tool; all guidance point numbers come from the ORC WG mirror, which states the Commission PDF is authoritative.

## Key question 2 — Article 24 open-source software stewards: definition, who qualifies, obligations, exemptions, guidance

### Takeaway
A steward is a legal person other than a manufacturer that systematically and sustainably supports FOSS "intended for commercial activities" without placing it on the market (Art 3(14)); foundations, not-for-profits and ordinary companies can all qualify, per project. Obligations are limited to a documented cybersecurity policy, cooperation with market surveillance authorities, and Art 14 reporting only to the extent the steward is involved in development or runs the development infrastructure (Art 24); stewards cannot CE-mark, need no conformity assessment, technical documentation or SBOM, and are exempt from administrative fines (Art 64(10)(b)). Section 3.3 (points 69–84) of the Commission's July 2026 guidance is the first official guidance on stewards; obligations apply from 11 December 2027.

### Cited Findings
- Art 3(14): "open-source software steward" = a legal person, other than a manufacturer, with the purpose or objective of systematically providing support on a sustained basis for the development of specific FOSS products intended for commercial activities, and that ensures their viability — [Enobyte mirror, Art 3](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/3/)
- Recital 19: stewards are legal persons providing sustained support for FOSS "intended for commercial activities" who "play a main role in ensuring the viability of those products"; they get a "light-touch and tailor-made regulatory regime" and "should not be permitted to affix the CE marking" — [EUR-Lex HTML, recitals](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847); [EUR-Lex PDF, recitals](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)
- Art 24(1): stewards shall put in place and document in a verifiable manner a cybersecurity policy to foster secure development and effective vulnerability handling by the developers; the policy shall take account of the steward's specific nature and legal/organisational arrangements, promote voluntary reporting under Art 15, and address documenting, addressing and remediating vulnerabilities and sharing information on discovered vulnerabilities within the open-source community — [Enobyte mirror, Art 24](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/24/)
- Art 24(2): stewards shall cooperate with market surveillance authorities at their request to mitigate cybersecurity risks and, on reasoned request, provide the Art 24(1) documentation in a language the authority can easily understand, in paper or electronic form — [Enobyte mirror, Art 24](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/24/)
- Art 24(3): Art 14(1) (notification of actively exploited vulnerabilities) applies to stewards "to the extent that they are involved in the development" of the products; Art 14(3) and (8) (severe incidents) apply to the extent severe incidents affect the network and information systems the steward provides for development — [Enobyte mirror, Art 24](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/24/)
- Art 64(10)(b): the administrative fines of Art 64(2)–(9) do not apply to "any infringement of this Regulation by open-source software stewards" (fine ceilings for others: EUR 15m/2.5% turnover for Annex I and Arts 13–14; EUR 10m/2%; EUR 5m/1%) — [Enobyte mirror, Art 64](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/64/); recital 120 says the same — [EUR-Lex HTML, recitals](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)
- Art 71: only Art 14 (from 11 Sept 2026) and Chapter IV Arts 35–51 (from 11 June 2026) are carved out of the general application date of 11 December 2027, so Art 24 applies from 11 December 2027 — [Enobyte mirror, Art 71](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/71/); the Linux Foundation playbook and OpenSSF state the same date for steward reporting — [LF CRA Stewards Playbook](https://policy.openssf.org/CRA/stewards-playbook.html); [OpenSSF, 11 Sept 2026](https://openssf.org/blog/2026/09/11/a-community-guide-to-the-eu-cra-september-11-deadline-for-manufacturers/)
- OpenSSF (11 Sept 2026): the 11 September 2026 reporting start creates no obligation for stewards; it cites the Commission and ENISA as having recently clarified this, without naming a document — [OpenSSF](https://openssf.org/blog/2026/09/11/a-community-guide-to-the-eu-cra-september-11-deadline-for-manufacturers/)
- Commission guidance points 69–74: the steward is a new category drawn from recital 19; it must be a legal person other than a manufacturer; stewardship applies only to FOSS not placed on the market; status is per project — the same entity can be a steward for one FOSS and a manufacturer for another — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Guidance point 75: sustained support can include hosting collaboration platforms or source code, governing projects and steering development; points 76–78: examples of stewards include entities integrating FOSS into their own products without placing it on the market, not-for-profits, and foundations offering sustained support — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Guidance point 79: support is non-technical (branding, governance, donations), infrastructure (hosting repositories, signing keys) or engineering (employing developers, managing releases, security patches), and obligations scale with the type of support under Art 24(3); points 80–82: non-technical-only stewards need not report actively exploited vulnerabilities but should share information per their policy; infrastructure providers must notify ENISA and CSIRTs of severe incidents affecting the infrastructure; stewards providing engineering resources must notify actively exploited vulnerabilities and inform users, directly where they have a direct relationship — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Guidance point 83: a steward that stops systematic support may cease to be a steward and is encouraged to communicate that; point 84: a steward that later monetises a project becomes its manufacturer from that date — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/); the LF playbook adds that manufacturer status does not attach to earlier versions — [LF CRA Stewards Playbook](https://policy.openssf.org/CRA/stewards-playbook.html)
- Guidance Examples 28–33: a not-for-profit foundation sustaining FOSS used by companies is the steward and those companies owe due diligence (Ex 28); Company A publishing unmonetised FOSS used by B, C, D is a steward, not a manufacturer (Ex 29); Company A selling a paid version with contributors from B, C, D is the manufacturer and B, C, D have no obligations for that FOSS (Ex 30); a not-for-profit publishing an SDK funded by membership fees is the steward and members are not responsible (Ex 33) — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Commission's FOSDEM 2024 examples of stewards, as recorded by the ORC WG FAQ: (1) foundations supporting specific FOSS projects, (2) companies that build FOSS for their own use and make it public, (3) not-for-profit entities developing FOSS; the FAQ also answers that a company (including a not-for-profit company) can be a steward, that a company can be manufacturer for a paid version and steward for the community version, and that one can be steward of one's own codebase — [ORC WG FAQ: stewards](https://cra.orcwg.org/faq/stewards/)
- Exemptions as summarised by the ecosystem: stewards owe no SBOM (a manufacturer obligation), no Art 31 technical documentation, and none of the Annex I essential requirements; they may provide these voluntarily through an Art 25 attestation programme — [ORC WG FAQ: stewards](https://cra.orcwg.org/faq/stewards/); the LF playbook states authorities may not require CE marking, conformity assessment, Annex VII technical documentation or SBOMs from stewards — [LF CRA Stewards Playbook](https://policy.openssf.org/CRA/stewards-playbook.html)
- Residual enforcement: a market surveillance authority finding non-compliance can require the steward to take corrective action under Art 52(3); the ORC WG expects enforcement to target governance and ecosystem risk rather than individual defects — [ORC WG FAQ: stewards](https://cra.orcwg.org/faq/stewards/); [ORC WG steward white paper V1.0, Jan 2026](https://orcwg.org/files/cra/resources/white-paper-on-open-source-software-stewards-and-cra.pdf)
- ORC WG "Open Source Software Stewards and CRA" white paper V1.0 (January 2026, Eclipse Foundation AISBL, CC-BY-SA 4.0, marked "not legal guidance"; contributors include Mikaël Barbero, Tobie Langel, Mark Thomas, Marta Rybczynska and others): recommends a verifiable policy adopted by projects, covering triage/prioritisation/remediation, information sharing with upstream and dependency maintainers, a channel for manufacturers to report vulnerabilities, voluntary CSIRT/ENISA reporting, evidence retained in version-controlled files (SECURITY.md, CVE/EUVD entries, advisories), reporting via the Single Reporting Platform and user notification preferably in machine-readable form such as CSAF; open questions include which CSIRT/MSA applies to stewards without EU establishment, whether ENISA should be a "CSIRT of last resort", and handling of AI-generated/low-quality reports — [ORC WG steward white paper PDF](https://orcwg.org/files/cra/resources/white-paper-on-open-source-software-stewards-and-cra.pdf); landing page status "Shipped" — [ORC D3.5 page](https://orcwg.org/cra/resources/d3-5-white-paper-on-open-source-software-stewards-and-cra/)
- Unresolved per CCPL (5 Aug 2026): the Commission did not clarify that entities providing only incidental support should be exempt from steward designation — [CCPL](https://www.centerforcybersecuritypolicy.org/insights-and-research/european-commission-publishes-final-cyber-resilience-act-implementation-guidance-addresses-concerns-raised-by-cybersecurity-coalition)
- FSFE Q&A with the German market surveillance authority and the Commission at FOSDEM 2026 (published 24 Feb 2026): "no one has to become a steward"; a manufacturer cannot force a project to take the role and may instead fork or find alternatives — [FSFE](https://fsfe.org/news/2026/news-20260224-01.html)

### Inferences
- The guidance's per-project test means an OSPO inside a commercial company is not itself a steward; the company (legal person) is the steward for each unmonetised FOSS project it sustains, and a manufacturer for products it sells. The guidance does not name OSPOs or public bodies.
- Because Art 24 applies only from 11 December 2027 and fines never apply, the practical pressure on stewards before then comes from manufacturers' Art 13(5) due diligence (from 11 December 2027) and from the voluntary reputational value of a published policy, not from enforcement.

### Gaps
- No standalone Commission or ENISA guidance document on stewards exists; the only official text is Section 3.3 of the July 2026 guidance plus the Commission FAQ (first published 3 December 2025 per the mirror; its steward content was not read).
- The guidance's status is described inconsistently: HLC and CCPL call it final/published on 27 July 2026; cyberresilienceact.eu says the content was approved on 27 July 2026 but formal adoption awaits all EU language versions. The mirror cites "C(2026) 5252 final" and point 8 says it is not binding.
- Which CSIRT a non-EU steward reports to remains open (ORC WG FAQ: "pending guidance").

## Key question 3 — Article 25 voluntary security attestation programmes: status of any delegated act and programmes

### Takeaway
Art 25 empowers the Commission to adopt delegated acts (under Art 61) establishing voluntary attestation programmes; as of 30 October 2026 I found no adopted or draft delegated act, no Commission programme, and no live programme from a foundation, ENISA or a national agency — only a Commission "study" referenced in February 2026 and ecosystem framework work (ORC WG two-tier proposal; D3.3 white paper still "Planned").

### Cited Findings
- Art 25 (single paragraph): to facilitate the Art 13(5) due diligence obligation, in particular for manufacturers integrating FOSS components, the Commission is empowered to adopt delegated acts under Art 61 establishing voluntary security attestation programmes allowing developers or users of FOSS products, and other third parties, to assess conformity with all or certain essential requirements or other obligations — [Enobyte mirror, Art 25](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/25/)
- Recital 21: programmes may be set up "either by a delegated act" or by requesting a European cybersecurity certification scheme (recital cites Article 48 [of the Cybersecurity Act — cross-reference not verified in the fetch]); they must take account of "the specificities of the free and open-source software development models"; attestations may be requested by manufacturers integrating the software, users and public administrations as well as developers — [EUR-Lex HTML, recitals](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847); recital 117 repeats the delegated-act empowerment — [EUR-Lex HTML, recitals](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)
- Commission FOSDEM slides (1 Feb 2026): mention "a study on voluntary security attestations" under Art 25; no delegated act or timing mentioned — [FOSDEM 2026 slides](https://archive.fosdem.org/2026/events/attachments/EERURR-implementing_the_cyber_resilience_act_-_engaging_with_open_source/slides/267519/slides-cr_pyebtat.pdf)
- FSFE (24 Feb 2026): attestation details are uncertain and "to be clarified by a delegated act"; FSFE ran a community survey on attestation closing 28 February 2026 to feed decision-makers — [FSFE](https://fsfe.org/news/2026/news-20260224-01.html)
- ORC WG D3.3 "White paper on security attestations": status "Planned", no date; may be merged with the due-diligence paper; will draw on the OpenJS Foundation's Ecosystem Sustainability Program as a case study — [ORC D3.3 page](https://orcwg.org/cra/resources/d3-3-white-paper-on-security-attestations/); [ORC resources list](https://orcwg.org/cra/resources/)
- ORC WG cra-attestations working document: two-tier framework adopted after the October 2025 Code&Compliance Workshop — Tier 1 a lightweight self-declaration aligned with BSI TR-03185-2, no third-party audit, ideally machine-readable; Tier 2 a proposed full conformity assessment (Module B+C or H) with possible third-party audit, not yet agreed; any market actor (stewards, researchers, manufacturers, national bodies) could author attestations; open items include verifying third-party attestations and market-distortion risk — [orcwg/cra-attestations overview](https://github.com/orcwg/cra-attestations/blob/main/overview.md)
- ORC WG FAQ: stewards may voluntarily join Art 25 attestation programmes, which can ease due diligence for manufacturers integrating attested components — [ORC WG FAQ: stewards](https://cra.orcwg.org/faq/stewards/)

### Inferences
- The task's phrase "implementing act" is a misnomer: Art 25 provides for delegated acts (Art 61), which carry Parliament/Council scrutiny; recital 21 also allows the CSA certification-scheme route. Either path appears to be at study stage only.
- With no official programme, manufacturers cannot yet rely on an "Art 25 attestation" as formal evidence; voluntary self-attestations (OpenSSF Baseline assessments, BSI TR-03185-2 self-declarations) are the de facto substitutes until a delegated act exists.

### Gaps
- No Commission page on the Art 25 study, its contractor or timeline was found; the Commission's "easily accessible list" of delegated/implementing acts (Art 26(2)) was not checked.
- No ENISA or national-agency (e.g. BSI, ANSSI) attestation programme for FOSS was found; absence of evidence, not confirmed absence.

## Key question 4 — Protections for individual contributors and maintainers; residual exposure

### Takeaway
Recital 18 and guidance points 48–49 and 85–86 exclude anyone contributing code to FOSS "not under their responsibility" — including people with commit rights — and the Commission ties responsibility to publishing and controlling releases. A natural person who publishes unmonetised FOSS has no CRA obligations at all (they cannot be a steward); exposure arises only if they monetise (manufacturer) or substantially modify FOSS they resell/integrate.

### Cited Findings
- Recital 18: the Regulation "does not apply to natural or legal persons who contribute with source code" to FOSS products not under their responsibility — [EUR-Lex HTML, recitals](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)
- Guidance points 48–49: establish whether the person actually supplies the FOSS; FOSS is "under the responsibility" of those who publish it and control releases (maintainers); commit access alone is not enough (Example 13: a contributor submitting a merge request to a maintainer-controlled project is not subject to the CRA) — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- Guidance points 85–86: the CRA does not apply to contributors to FOSS not under their responsibility; manufacturers integrating FOSS do not become responsible for those components' own compliance even when they contribute code — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- HLC on the final guidance: contributing code, including holding technical permissions or submitting a pull request, does not make someone a manufacturer or steward; a contributor without primary decision-making authority over releases or governance has no CRA obligations for that contribution — [HLC](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/eu-cyber-resilience-act-european-commission-publishes-final-cra-guidance)
- Guidance point 53: a natural person supplying a free community version is outside the CRA (only legal persons can be stewards); point 58: natural persons charging only to recover actual costs including reasonable living expenses are not placing on the market; Example 34: an individual publishing a library on a package repository with a donation link — neither the developer nor the repository has obligations, the integrating manufacturer owes due diligence — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- ORC WG FAQ: not all projects have a steward because a steward must be a legal person, and most projects are not backed by a company or foundation — [ORC WG FAQ: stewards](https://cra.orcwg.org/faq/stewards/)
- FSFE: the CRA is product regulation on manufacturers and does not apply to individuals or projects that are not manufacturers; FSFE will focus on protecting contributors and small projects — [FSFE](https://fsfe.org/news/2026/news-20260224-01.html)
- Debian General Resolution (vote 9–22 Dec 2023): Option 1 ("CRA and PLD proposals include regulations detrimental to FOSS") won; it argued open development should be exempt like private development, that 24-hour reporting would undermine responsible disclosure, and that liability would push self-employed developers out — a position formed on the pre-final text — [Debian GR 2023/002](https://www.debian.org/vote/2023/vote_002)

### Inferences
- Residual exposure for individual maintainers is confined to: (a) monetising access, updates or support in ways points 51–57 and 62 treat as a price (manufacturer status, full CRA, fines possible); (b) acting as a service provider who substantially modifies FOSS for a customer (Art 22, point 59); (c) non-CRA regimes (the revised Product Liability Directive is outside this note's scope). Within the CRA, a non-monetising natural person has no obligations and no fine exposure.
- Maintainers will nonetheless feel indirect pressure: Art 13(6) obliges manufacturers to report vulnerabilities to them and share fixes, and manufacturers' due diligence will favour projects with a security policy and a steward.

### Gaps
- The interaction with the revised Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 for FOSS supplied outside a commercial activity was not researched (out of scope here).

## Key question 5 — Manufacturers consuming open-source components: Art 13(5)–(7), SBOM, Art 32(5), attestations as evidence

### Takeaway
A manufacturer integrating FOSS remains fully responsible for its own product: it must exercise due diligence on third-party components including unplaced FOSS (Art 13(5)), report vulnerabilities it finds in a component to the maintainer and share any fix it develops, machine-readable where appropriate (Art 13(6)), document vulnerabilities and third-party information (Art 13(7)), and include components in its SBOM (Annex I Part II(1)); a manufacturer that places FOSS on the market commercially as an Annex III "important" product may use any conformity procedure including internal control if it publishes its technical documentation (Art 32(5)).

### Cited Findings
- Art 13(5): manufacturers "shall exercise due diligence when integrating components sourced from third parties" so they do not compromise the product's cybersecurity, "including when integrating components of free and open-source software" that have not been made available on the market in the course of a commercial activity — [Enobyte mirror, Art 13](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/13/)
- Art 13(6): on identifying a vulnerability in a component including an open-source component, the manufacturer shall "report the vulnerability to the person or entity manufacturing or maintaining the component", address and remediate it per Annex I Part II, and where it develops a fix "share the relevant code or documentation" with that person or entity, "where appropriate in a machine-readable format" — [Enobyte mirror, Art 13](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/13/)
- Art 13(7): manufacturers shall systematically document, proportionately to the nature and risks, relevant cybersecurity aspects "including vulnerabilities of which they become aware and any relevant information provided by third parties" and update the risk assessment where applicable; the SBOM requirement sits in Annex I Part II point (1), cross-referenced from Art 13 — [Enobyte mirror, Art 13](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/13/)
- Recital 77: manufacturers should identify and document components including by drawing up an SBOM; "Manufacturers should not be obliged to make the SBOM public" — [EUR-Lex HTML, recitals](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847)
- Art 32(5): manufacturers of Annex III important products that qualify as FOSS may demonstrate conformity using any procedure in Art 32(1) (i.e. including internal control, module A), "provided that the technical documentation referred to in Article 31 is made available to the public" when the product is placed on the market — [Enobyte mirror, Art 32](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/32/); recital 91 confirms the internal-control option for important FOSS products with public technical documentation — [EUR-Lex HTML, recitals](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847); the Commission's FOSDEM slides say open-source products may use self-assessment whether or not important — [FOSDEM 2026 slides](https://archive.fosdem.org/2026/events/attachments/EERURR-implementing_the_cyber_resilience_act_-_engaging_with_open_source/slides/267519/slides-cr_pyebtat.pdf)
- Guidance points 86–88: manufacturers integrating FOSS do not become responsible for the component's own compliance; whether the CRA applies to the component depends only on whether its publisher places it on the market; the integrator must comply for its own product, exercise Art 13(5) due diligence, and report vulnerabilities and share fixes under Art 13(6) (cross-reference to Section 9.2.1) — [ORC WG mirror of Commission guidance](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/)
- HLC: the guidance's paragraphs 167–177 cover handling third-party and open-source components in risk assessments (no detail given) — [HLC](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/eu-cyber-resilience-act-european-commission-publishes-final-cra-guidance)
- Commission FOSDEM slides: integrators must provide maintainers of open-source components with available fixes; manufacturers must report vulnerabilities to upstream developers and provide security fixes free of charge — [FOSDEM 2026 slides](https://archive.fosdem.org/2026/events/attachments/EERURR-implementing_the_cyber_resilience_act_-_engaging_with_open_source/slides/267519/slides-cr_pyebtat.pdf)
- OpenSSF (11 Sept 2026): where a manufacturer traces an actively exploited vulnerability to a FOSS component it remains responsible for Art 14 reporting but may engage the project/steward; Art 13(6) fix-sharing is mandatory for manufacturers but is not part of the 11 September 2026 milestone (it applies from 11 Dec 2027) — [OpenSSF](https://openssf.org/blog/2026/09/11/a-community-guide-to-the-eu-cra-september-11-deadline-for-manufacturers/)
- Linux Foundation Research survey (blog 27 March 2025): only 34% of manufacturers produce comprehensive SBOMs; 46% passively rely on upstream projects for security fixes; manufacturers who made assessments expect an average 6% price increase — [Linux Foundation](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/blog/unaware-and-uncertain-is-the-open-source-community-prepared-for-the-new-regulatory-reality-of-the-cyber-resilience-act)
- ORC WG D3.2 "White paper on due diligence obligation of manufacturers": status "Work in Progress" (resources page last updated September 2026) — [ORC resources list](https://orcwg.org/cra/resources/)

### Inferences
- The text of Art 13(5) does not itself enumerate due-diligence steps (known exploitable vulnerabilities, maintenance status, monitoring); those expectations come from the Commission guidance's Section 9.2.1 (points ~167–177), which I could not read, and from ecosystem checklists (ORC D3.2, OpenSSF manufacturer checklist). The report writer should present them as guidance/best practice, not statutory wording.
- Art 25 attestations are by design the evidence route for due diligence ("to facilitate the due diligence obligation"), but with no programme in force, a manufacturer's evidence today is its own component assessment plus any voluntary self-declaration a project publishes.

### Gaps
- Section 9.2.1 (points ~167–177) of the Commission guidance on component due diligence could not be read: the ORC mirror fetches returned points 1–131 and a fragment near the end; the Commission PDF returned no text. Its specific due-diligence steps and any treatment of attestations as evidence remain unverified.
- Annex I Part II(1) SBOM wording (top-level dependencies at minimum, commonly used machine-readable format) was not fetched directly; recital 77 and the Art 13 mirror confirm only the existence and non-publication points.

## Key question 7 — Decision table: open-source situation → role under the CRA → obligations

### Takeaway
The table consolidates the legal text and the Commission's July 2026 guidance (point and Example numbers refer to C(2026) 5252 as mirrored by the ORC WG). Natural persons who do not monetise are always outside the CRA; legal persons are stewards when they sustain unmonetised FOSS "intended for commercial activities" and manufacturers when they monetise; integrators always owe due diligence for their own products.

### Cited Findings
| Open-source situation | Role under the CRA | Obligations | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual publishes FOSS, no monetisation (donation link allowed) | Outside the CRA (not placed on market; natural persons cannot be stewards) | None | Guidance pts 53, 58, 60–61; Ex 34 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| Individual or company contributes code (PR, commit rights) to a project it does not publish/control | Not a manufacturer or steward | None for that contribution | Recital 18 — [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847); pts 48–49, 85–86; Ex 13 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| Individual charges for binaries/updates, or only donors get releases/fixes | Manufacturer | Full CRA (Annex I, Arts 13–14, CE marking); fines possible | Pts 51, 57, 62; Ex 21–22 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| Individual charges only cost recovery incl. reasonable living expenses | Not placed on market | None | Pt 58 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| Not-for-profit foundation sustains FOSS used commercially; earnings reinvested | Steward | Art 24 only, from 11 Dec 2027; no CE marking, SBOM, tech docs or fines | Recital 18–19; pt 66, 76–78; Ex 24, 28, 32–33 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/); Art 64(10)(b) — [mirror Art 64](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/64/) |
| Company publishes unmonetised FOSS it also integrates into its own products (incl. internally developed software released as open source) | Steward for the FOSS; manufacturer for its own products | Art 24 for the FOSS; full CRA for the products | Pts 67–68, 75–78; Ex 25, 29, 31 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| Open core: paid/enterprise edition plus free community edition | Manufacturer for paid edition; steward for community edition (if legal person) | Full CRA for paid edition; Art 24 for community edition | Pts 52–53; Ex 17, 30 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/); [ORC FAQ](https://cra.orcwg.org/faq/stewards/) |
| Paid consulting/training/support sold separately; software and maintenance freely accessible | Not placed on market (steward if sustained support by legal person) | Art 24 if steward; otherwise none | Pts 55–56; Ex 18 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| Paid access to a specific version bundled with support/optimisation | Manufacturer for that version | Full CRA | Pt 57; Ex 17 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| FOSS monetised via ads, commissions, upsold services or mandatory personal-data processing | Manufacturer | Full CRA | Recital 15 — [EUR-Lex PDF](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:L_202402847); pt 54; Ex 14–16 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| Developer paid by grant/bounty/company to build an openly shared feature | Not placed on market; funder/integrator owes due diligence | None for developer; Art 13(5) for integrator | Pts 63–65; Ex 23, 27 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| Repository/package manager/collaboration platform hosting FOSS | Not making available on market; not a distributor unless supplying commercially | None | Recital 20 — [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847); Ex 34 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| Service provider installs/configures FOSS for a customer without substantial modification | Not placing on market | None (Art 22 applies if substantially modified) | Pt 59; Ex 19 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| Public administration supplies FOSS and charges only actual-cost recovery | Not commercial on that ground alone | None on that basis | Recital 16 — [EUR-Lex PDF](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=OJ:L_202402847) |
| Manufacturer integrates an unplaced FOSS component into a commercial product | Manufacturer for own product; not responsible for the component's own compliance | Art 13(5) due diligence; Art 13(6) report vulnerabilities to maintainer and share fixes; Art 13(7) documentation; SBOM (Annex I Part II(1)) | [mirror Art 13](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/13/); pts 86–88 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| Manufacturer places FOSS on the market commercially and it is an Annex III important product | Manufacturer | May use any Art 32(1) procedure incl. internal control if technical documentation is made public | Art 32(5) — [mirror Art 32](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/32/); recital 91 — [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402847) |
| Steward providing non-technical support only (branding, governance, donations) | Steward | Policy (Art 24(1)); cooperation (Art 24(2)); no Art 14 reporting | Pt 80 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/); [ORC FAQ](https://cra.orcwg.org/faq/stewards/) |
| Steward also providing infrastructure (repos, CI, signing keys) | Steward | + report severe incidents affecting that infrastructure (Art 24(3) via Art 14(3),(8)) and inform users | Pt 81 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/); [mirror Art 24](https://enobyte.com/en/legal/cra/24/) |
| Steward also providing engineering/release/security resources | Steward | + report actively exploited vulnerabilities (Art 24(3) via Art 14(1)); inform users directly where there is a direct relationship | Pt 82 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |
| Steward starts monetising the project | Becomes manufacturer from that date (not for earlier versions) | Full CRA for versions placed from then | Pt 84 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/); [LF playbook](https://policy.openssf.org/CRA/stewards-playbook.html) |
| Steward stops systematic support | Ceases to be steward; encouraged to communicate | None after cessation | Pt 83 — [mirror](https://cra.orcwg.org/official-guidance/) |

### Inferences
- An OSPO is never itself the regulated entity; the legal person (company or foundation) is, assessed project by project.
- A financial-services firm that consumes FOSS but does not publish it is only ever in the "manufacturer integrating a component" row (if it places products with digital elements on the EU market) or outside the CRA entirely (if it only uses software internally and places nothing on the market).

### Gaps
- Dual licensing, paid hosting of FOSS and public bodies acting as stewards are not addressed in the guidance text I could read; rows for those scenarios are omitted rather than inferred.
